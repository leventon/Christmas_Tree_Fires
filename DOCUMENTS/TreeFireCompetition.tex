\abstract{Each year since 2014, a Christmas tree fire safety event has been organized at the University of Maryland to highlight the potential fire risk of dry Christmas trees. Full-scale fire experiments at these events are used to demonstrate how properly maintaining a cut Christmas tree (to maintain a high moisture content in tree branches and needles) can help to reduce the likelihood of  and to limit fire growth rate and peak fire size. The event is regularly covered by local and national media who help to distribute and amplify the holiday fire safety message. Additionally, the experiments provide in-person experience with large fire dynamics for fire protection engineering students (to support their coursework), they have supported** advanced diagnostics for multiple research projects, and they have enabled an annual, friendly competition that encourages communication and collaboration between members of the fire safety science community. This manuscript introduces a new digital repository that organizes and maintains test data and metadata from 15 years of these experiments. It also quantifies factors impacting fire growth behavior in these tests and offers an analysis of how well competition participants (members of the fire safety science and engineering community) can predict ignition and fire growth behavior of these tree fires.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Every year, fire departments in the United States respond to an average of 150 to 200 Christmas Tree fires~\cite{Campbell2022christmas} (despite their use in a relatively short time each year).Home structure fires involving Christmas trees are, on average, substantially more deadly\footnote{A 2017 the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) report, \textit{Home Structure Fires Involving Christmas Trees}~\cite{ahrens2017christmastrees}, noted that, "If a Christmas tree fire occurs, the risk of death is unusually high... On average, one of every 32 reported home Christmas tree fires resulted in a death, compared to an average of one death per 143 total reported home fires." This represents a factor of 4.5x increase.} than other house fires~\cite{ahrens2017christmastrees}. Beginning in 2014~\cite{pbsnewshour2014christmastrees}, a series of Christmas Tree fire safety demonstrations has been coordinated each year to: (1) support and promote better public awareness of fire safety during the holiday season, (2) to highlight relevant experimental\cite{Giovanni's work, Gollner/Leventon} and modeling\cite{Mell, Maranghides, McDermott} research projects and tools, and (3) to offer Fire Protection Engineering students the opportunity to experience and measure fire behaviors that they [typically only study on paper during their regular coursework**]. These tests are conducted at the University of Maryland, College Park (UMD) with support from faculty and researchers of the UMD Department of Fire Protection Engineering (FPE) and the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST).

In its first year, this fire safety event served as the final for an introductory fire science program taught at UMD (this program was supported in 2015 by a grant from the Society of Fire Protection Engineers, SFPE, Scientific and Educational Foundation). Later, senior- and graduate-level students of the FPE department have watched these fire experiments (from a safe distance) to contextualize the fire behaviors that they study. In various years, these experiments were included in courses such as fire dynamics, enclosure fire modeling, and the graduate fire dynamics laboratory to allow students to practice assessing design fires, calculating fire risk (e.g., zone of influence and heat transfer at a distance), and performing statistical and uncertainty analyses of large fire datasets.

As the risk posed by wildfires and wildland urban interface (WUI) fires continues to grow~\cite{parks2020warmer, cunningham2024increasing,kelley2025state}, test matrices for these fire safety demonstrations have been expanded and additional measurement devices have been incorporated to produce unique datasets (e.g., firebrand yield) and to quantify factors that impact tree burning behavior (e.g., ignition source, ambient conditions, or tree species, size, and moisture content). In some cases, these results have been directly included in conference presentations and/or journal publications~\cite{leventon-gollner}; in others, the experiments provide an opportunity for calibration measurement devices used in other studies~\cite{adetolla-raffan}.

In addition to sharing a fire safety message and supporting research efforts, these experiments also serve as the target for an annual competition to predict the burning behavior (i.e., heat release rate, HRR) of Christmas Tree fires, from ignition to final burnout. This competition provides a platform for open communication between members of the fire safety science community and an opportunity to encourage and motivate current researchers and the next generation of scientists and engineers to explore and develop collaborations related to emerging areas of fire safety science. 

This paper highlights the development of these fire safety demos from a small, local event within the Department of Fire Protection Engineering at UMD to annual tradition with a global footprint: each year, the Christmas Tree Fire Size Competition includes hundreds predictions from participants in dozens of countries across the world and [related fire safety multi-media/videos] have received millions of impressions while the fire safety experiments receive continued local and national media attention. Specifically, this manuscript describes the development, organization, and standard formatting used in a new online repository 
of the measurement data and test metadata obtained during these experiments. Corresponding analysis of these results highlights impacts of key factors that affect observed burning behavior. A review of competition results similarly highlights how the HRR prediction competition is organized, how it has grown, and what can be learned from these predictions (i.e., what are participants - all members of the fire safety science and engineering community - good or bad at predicting and (how) has this evolved?).

Christmas tree fire safety research has been conducted for decades, demonstrating the impact of moisture content~\cite{stroup1999scotch,damant1994christmas,Babrauskas2001, white1997flammability}, geometry~\cite{madrzykowski2008impact}, tree size~\cite{evans2004physics}, and even the impact of residential sprinklers\cite{madrzykowski2008impact} on tree burning behavior. \subsection{Measurements and Test Procedure}\label{ssec:exp-measurements}
After procurement, the initial mass, height, and weight of each tree is measured and then trees are kept unwatered for approximately 14 to 21 days in laboratory storage at the University of Maryland\footnote{Each year, at least one tree is kept in a 5 gallon bucket of water (continuously refilled) to allow for comparison of well-watered and dry tree burning behavior. In some years, additional tests are conducted with expanded conditioning durations to explore a wider range of tree moisture contents. All trees are stored approximately at approximately 40\% relative humidity and 20$^\circ$C}. Immediately prior to testing, small branches are removed from the base of each tree (approximately 100~g to 200~g), weighed, dried in an oven at 80$^\circ$C for approximately 2 days, and then re-weighed. Tree moisture content is calculated as $MC=\frac{m_{0}-m_{\text{dry}}}{m_\text{{dry}}}$.%, where the subscripts '0' and 'dry' indicate branch masses before and after oven drying.
The 'dry' mass of the full tree is then measured, the tree is secured in a metal stand, and then this stand is placed above a mass balance that is protected by a radiation shield and several layers of rigid thermal insulation. 

During tests, the mass balance/tree are positioned at the center of (underneath) an exhaust hood that measures 3.66~m by 3.66~m (square profile, sides nominally 12 ft wide). The hood is rated for a nominal air flow rate of 10,000~CFM. Three sides of the exhaust hood were enclosed using fire-rated fabric curtains to help contain the potential spread of firebrands generated during testing.  When used, heat flux gauges and/or thermocouples are then secured in place and their locations are measured/recorded. The ignition source is then moved into position below the lowest branches of the tree, data and video recording are started and confirmed, and the ignition source is ignited. Once the lower branches of the tree ignite, the exhaust hood is turned on. Trees are allowed to burn until self-extinction, after which final tree mass is measured, data and video recording were stopped, and pictures of the remaining sample were taken.

\subsubsection{Ignition Source}
Previous studies have demonstrated how ignition source type, strength, duration, and location all can affect fire growth characteristics of both vegetative fuels and materials/products found in the built environment~\cite{dembsey1993effect,ai1987effects,cleary1994influence, Babrauskas2001, Morandini2019, Accary2024, gould2022initial, Babrauskas1985Fire}. In a wildfire context, multiple studies have also assessed the impact of ignition source (e.g., point source vs. smaller or larger line fires) on wildfire spread rate~\cite{Rothermel1984Aerial, Johansen1987Ignition, Alexander1991PorterLake,gould2022initial}. 
Though this has been explored in some modeling studies (e.g., Ref~\cite{Accary2024}, limited information is available from experiments designed to systematically quantify how variations in ignition size/severity, duration, and/or location affect fire growth rate/peak fire size of individual vegetative fuels (which in turn controls/can strongly impact ROS).

With this in mind, careful consideration was given to the ignition sources used in this Christmas Tree fire safety study. This ensured that ignition could be performed repeatably and that the source reasonably represented a small fire hazard found in the home. Most often, this meant filling a 6~cm to 10~cm disc (cut from rigid ceramic insulation board and backed/wrapped by aluminum foil) with approximately 30~mL of heptane and placing this beneath the lowest branches at the base of the tree. Both to conduct parametric studies and to make the fire size prediction competition more challenging (i.e., such that ignition and fire growth could be reasonably expected to change each year) the location of this small pool fire could be moved from year to year (e.g., near the center of the tree or towards the outer branches). Also, in some years, this pool fire was replaced by a full ring (cut from the same ceramic insulation) to allow for uniform ignition around the entire base of the tree. Additionally, in later years of the competition, tree ignition was accomplished by the spread of a ground fire across an angled bed of excelsior placed below the base of the tree - this new ignition source both (1) offered a new connection to wildland fire scenarios and (2) emphasized prediction of tree ignition time delay (due to flame spread) as an important scoring category.

\subsubsection{Measurement Devices}
\textbf{Burning Rate and Heat Release}\\
Measurements recorded during this ongoing test series include: initial and final tree masses, time-resolved mass loss rate during burning, and heat flux at a distance. In certain test series, local temperature measurements (in or near the tree during burning) or total firebrand yield and mass/projected areas of individual firebrands were also recorded. In general, all experiments were conducted in the large fire lab at the University of Maryland. However, in 2024, the competition was based on measurements of extremely large (5.8~m** tall) tree fires that were performed in the National Fire Research Laborartory (NFRL) at NIST. This measurements and corresponding uncertainties of this dataset -- including MLR**, HRR, heat flux at \#** locations -- is detailed elsewhere~\cite{Gio et al.}. Excluding this dataset, all measurements were recorded as described below.

A Mettler Toldeo **Model Number* mass balance \footnote{ The identification of any commercial product or trade name does not imply endorsement or recommendation by NIST (or any other contributing institution)} with a 32~kg range and 0.1~g precision was used for all\footnote{In Winter 2019/2020, a detailed study of firebrand generation was conducted; the masses of individual firebrands was measured using a balance with higher precision, as described below.} mass measurements obtained in this test series (both static, and time-resolved). Sample mass was continuously measured at 1~Hz throughout the duration of each experiment. Mass loss rate was then calculated at each time step, $t_i$, as the numerical derivative of sample mass curves across a $\pm$~2~s interval, as follows: 
\begin{equation}
	\dot{m}_{i} = \frac{\left(m_{i-2}-m_{i+2}\right)}{\left(t_{i+2}-t_{i-2}\right)}
\end{equation}

For the purposes of the competition, heat release rate was calculated as the product of measured mass loss rate and an assumed heat of combustion, typically $\Delta H_c=17.7$~kJ/g.

\section{Cross referencing}\label{sec8}

Environments such as figure, table, equation and align can have a label
declared via the \verb+\label{#label}+ command. For figures and table
environments use the \verb+\label{}+ command inside or just
below the \verb+\caption{}+ command. You can then use the
\verb+\ref{#label}+ command to cross-reference them. As an example, consider
the label declared for Figure~\ref{fig1} which is
\verb+\label{fig1}+. To cross-reference it, use the command 
\verb+Figure \ref{fig1}+, for which it comes up as
``Figure~\ref{fig1}''. 

To reference line numbers in an algorithm, consider the label declared for the line number 2 of Algorithm~\ref{algo1} is \verb+\label{algln2}+. To cross-reference it, use the command \verb+\ref{algln2}+ for which it comes up as line~\ref{algln2} of Algorithm~\ref{algo1}.

\textbf{Ethical approval declarations} (only required where applicable) Any article reporting experiment/s carried out on (i)~live vertebrate (or higher invertebrates), (ii)~humans or (iii)~human samples must include an unambiguous statement within the methods section that meets the following requirements: 

\begin{enumerate}[1.]
\item Approval: a statement which confirms that all experimental protocols were approved by a named institutional and/or licensing committee. Please identify the approving body in the methods section

\item Accordance: a statement explicitly saying that the methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations

\item Informed consent (for experiments involving humans or human tissue samples): include a statement confirming that informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardian/s
\end{enumerate}
