\abstract{Each year since 2014, a Christmas tree fire safety event has been organized at the University of Maryland to highlight the potential fire risk of dry Christmas trees. Full-scale fire experiments at these events are used to demonstrate how properly maintaining a cut Christmas tree (to maintain a high moisture content in tree branches and needles) can help to reduce the likelihood of  and to limit fire growth rate and peak fire size. The event is regularly covered by local and national media who help to distribute and amplify the holiday fire safety message. Additionally, the experiments provide in-person experience with large fire dynamics for fire protection engineering students (to support their coursework), they have supported** advanced diagnostics for multiple research projects, and they have enabled an annual, friendly competition that encourages communication and collaboration between members of the fire safety science community. This manuscript introduces a new digital repository that organizes and maintains test data and metadata from 15 years of these experiments. It also quantifies factors impacting fire growth behavior in these tests and offers an analysis of how well competition participants (members of the fire safety science and engineering community) can predict ignition and fire growth behavior of these tree fires.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Every year, fire departments in the United States respond to an average of 150 to 200 Christmas Tree fires~\cite{Campbell2022christmas} (despite their use in a relatively short time each year).Home structure fires involving Christmas trees are, on average, substantially more deadly\footnote{A 2017 the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) report, \textit{Home Structure Fires Involving Christmas Trees}~\cite{ahrens2017christmastrees}, noted that, "If a Christmas tree fire occurs, the risk of death is unusually high... On average, one of every 32 reported home Christmas tree fires resulted in a death, compared to an average of one death per 143 total reported home fires." This represents a factor of 4.5x increase.} than other house fires~\cite{ahrens2017christmastrees}. Beginning in 2014~\cite{pbsnewshour2014christmastrees}, a series of Christmas Tree fire safety demonstrations has been coordinated each year to: (1) support and promote better public awareness of fire safety during the holiday season, (2) to highlight relevant experimental\cite{Giovanni's work, Gollner/Leventon} and modeling\cite{Mell, Maranghides, McDermott} research projects and tools, and (3) to offer Fire Protection Engineering students the opportunity to experience and measure fire behaviors that they [typically only study on paper during their regular coursework**]. These tests are conducted at the University of Maryland, College Park (UMD) with support from faculty and researchers of the UMD Department of Fire Protection Engineering (FPE) and the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST).

In its first year, this fire safety event served as the final for an introductory fire science program taught at UMD (this program was supported in 2015 by a grant from the Society of Fire Protection Engineers, SFPE, Scientific and Educational Foundation). Later, senior- and graduate-level students of the FPE department have watched these fire experiments (from a safe distance) to contextualize the fire behaviors that they study. In various years, these experiments were included in courses such as fire dynamics, enclosure fire modeling, and the graduate fire dynamics laboratory to allow students to practice assessing design fires, calculating fire risk (e.g., zone of influence and heat transfer at a distance), and performing statistical and uncertainty analyses of large fire datasets.

As the risk posed by wildfires and wildland urban interface (WUI) fires continues to grow~\cite{parks2020warmer, cunningham2024increasing,kelley2025state}, test matrices for these fire safety demonstrations have been expanded and additional measurement devices have been incorporated to produce unique datasets (e.g., firebrand yield) and to quantify factors that impact tree burning behavior (e.g., ignition source, ambient conditions, or tree species, size, and moisture content). In some cases, these results have been directly included in conference presentations and/or journal publications~\cite{leventon-gollner}; in others, the experiments provide an opportunity for calibration measurement devices used in other studies~\cite{adetolla-raffan}.

In addition to sharing a fire safety message and supporting research efforts, these experiments also serve as the target for an annual competition to predict the burning behavior (i.e., heat release rate, HRR) of Christmas Tree fires, from ignition to final burnout. This competition provides a platform for open communication between members of the fire safety science community and an opportunity to encourage and motivate current researchers and the next generation of scientists and engineers to explore and develop collaborations related to emerging areas of fire safety science. 

This paper highlights the development of these fire safety demos from a small, local event within the Department of Fire Protection Engineering at UMD to annual tradition with a global footprint: each year, the Christmas Tree Fire Size Competition includes hundreds predictions from participants in dozens of countries across the world and [related fire safety multi-media/videos] have received millions of impressions while the fire safety experiments receive continued local and national media attention. Specifically, this manuscript describes the development, organization, and standard formatting used in a new online repository 
of the measurement data and test metadata obtained during these experiments. Corresponding analysis of these results highlights impacts of key factors that affect observed burning behavior. A review of competition results similarly highlights how the HRR prediction competition is organized, how it has grown, and what can be learned from these predictions (i.e., what are participants - all members of the fire safety science and engineering community - good or bad at predicting and (how) has this evolved?).

\section{Cross referencing}\label{sec8}

Environments such as figure, table, equation and align can have a label
declared via the \verb+\label{#label}+ command. For figures and table
environments use the \verb+\label{}+ command inside or just
below the \verb+\caption{}+ command. You can then use the
\verb+\ref{#label}+ command to cross-reference them. As an example, consider
the label declared for Figure~\ref{fig1} which is
\verb+\label{fig1}+. To cross-reference it, use the command 
\verb+Figure \ref{fig1}+, for which it comes up as
``Figure~\ref{fig1}''. 

To reference line numbers in an algorithm, consider the label declared for the line number 2 of Algorithm~\ref{algo1} is \verb+\label{algln2}+. To cross-reference it, use the command \verb+\ref{algln2}+ for which it comes up as line~\ref{algln2} of Algorithm~\ref{algo1}.
